\projectsection{1. Timeline Entry}
\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Date:} 1935--1940\par
\textbf{Title:} Federal Housing Systems Classify Rochester Neighborhoods\par
\textbf{Brief description:} During the 1930s, federal housing systems translated racial, class, and immigrant prejudice into official judgments about neighborhood risk. The Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) created color-coded ``residential security'' maps between 1935 and 1940, while the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) used its own underwriting rules to decide which mortgages would receive federal insurance. In Rochester, these systems helped normalize unequal access to credit and investment. Their legacy matters to climate justice because decades of unequal public and private investment shaped the housing, infrastructure, green space, and resources available to neighborhoods as climate risks intensified. Institutional records, however, are only part of the history: Clarissa Street oral histories and business records document a Black community rich in entrepreneurship, culture, relationships, joy, and resistance---qualities erased by labels such as ``hazardous.''
\end{focusbox}

\subsectiontitle{Historical precision and limits}
The famous HOLC maps should not be treated as a single cause of every later inequality. Mapping Inequality explains that most HOLC refinancing loans had already been issued before its City Survey maps were produced. The FHA operated a separate underwriting and mortgage-insurance system whose 1938 manual referenced racially discriminatory policies. Students therefore investigate related institutions without collapsing them into one agency or claiming that a map alone proves a direct causal line to every present outcome. This distinction is essential to evidence-based justice work: a stronger explanation names the policy mechanisms, identifies what the records can support, and states what remains uncertain.

\subsectiontitle{Why this belongs in a climate-justice timeline}
Redlining is usually introduced as housing history, but it also helps students ask how past decisions distribute present exposure and protection. National research has found hotter land-surface temperatures in formerly redlined neighborhoods and connects those patterns partly to impervious surfaces and lower tree canopy (Hoffman, Shandas, \& Pendleton, 2020). Rochester-specific tree-cover work and the prior ED400/ED415 Resistance Mapping inquiry make the local question concrete: which neighborhoods received investment, shade, mobility, healthy environments, and institutional credibility, and whose knowledge can help explain the pattern? The timeline entry does not assert that redlining alone produced Rochester's current climate inequities. It establishes a documented historical layer students can test against additional local evidence.

\subsectiontitle{Accessibility and source preparation}
The activity keeps every original source visible and gives students several supported routes into the same disciplinary work:
\begin{itemize}
  \item crop and enlarge the Rochester map while retaining its title, legend, date, creator, and link to the full interactive map;
  \item provide high-contrast printed and digital versions, alt text describing color grades and spatial relationships, and avoid relying on color names alone;
  \item use one short FHA excerpt with original pagination beside a teacher-written plain-language note and vocabulary for \emph{underwriting}, \emph{mortgage insurance}, \emph{risk rating}, and \emph{redlining};
  \item provide transcripts/captions for oral-history clips and allow replay, read-aloud, or silent transcript reading;
  \item let students record their evidence trail in writing, an annotated map, or a brief audio explanation while requiring the same source citations and reasoning.
\end{itemize}
These are access supports, not simplified conclusions. Students still source, contextualize, corroborate, and qualify their claims.

This design also reflects my Puzzle Plan commitment to students whose identities and access needs overlap. A multilingual or culturally diverse student may also be neurodivergent, chronically ill, disabled, an immigrant, or navigating unfamiliar institutional language. Those parts of identity do not take turns. Short source segments, explicit vocabulary, predictable steps, captions, processing time, and multiple ways to explain an evidence trail reduce barriers that can hide historical reasoning. They do not presume that every student in a group needs the same support, and they do not lower the expectation that every interpretation must be grounded in evidence.


\projectsection{3. Historical-Thinking Task}
\begin{focusbox}
\textbf{Task title:} Reading the Map Against the Community Record\par
\textbf{Suggested setting:} Grades 8--10; one 50-minute period\par
\textbf{Compelling question:} What can an institutional map tell us about a neighborhood, and what does it leave out?\par
\textbf{Student product:} A qualified claim supported by at least two sources from different positions, plus one stated limitation or unresolved question.
\end{focusbox}

\subsectiontitle{Learning goals}
Students will:
\begin{itemize}
  \item source a map, policy document, oral history, community record, and news account by identifying creator, purpose, audience, time, and position;
  \item distinguish an institution's classification from evidence about how people lived, built community, and resisted;
  \item contextualize and corroborate across sources instead of assuming that ``primary'' means automatically reliable;
  \item make a defensible historical claim and qualify what the evidence cannot establish;
  \item connect historical housing systems to a climate-justice question without treating correlation as automatic proof of causation.
  \item use a supported response route to make historical reasoning visible without confusing language, disability, health, or processing barriers with an absence of disciplinary thinking.
\end{itemize}

\subsectiontitle{Sequence}
\textbf{1. Notice and wonder --- 7 minutes.} Display the Rochester HOLC map without its explanatory caption. Students record two observations and two questions. The teacher then reveals the title, date, creator, legend, and purpose. Students revise one initial inference.

\textbf{2. Source and contextualize --- 10 minutes.} In pairs, students examine Source A and the selected Source B excerpt. They answer: Who created this? For what institutional decision? What words or categories reveal the source's assumptions? What can this document answer, and what can it not answer?

\textbf{3. Read against the institutional record --- 12 minutes.} Pairs examine one Clarissa Uprooted oral-history transcript segment and the Black-owned-business map/directory. They add evidence to a two-column organizer: \emph{What the institution claimed or valued} / \emph{What the community record makes visible}. They note that a community source has a perspective while still carrying historical evidence.

\textbf{4. Corroborate and verify --- 8 minutes.} Students use a compact SIFT routine on Source E: stop; investigate WHEC and the reporter; find stronger coverage in Sources A--D; trace one claim to its original context. They identify one place where the article is supported and one place where it compresses distinct housing systems or limits community voice.

\textbf{5. Evidence-based judgment --- 10 minutes.} Students respond to the compelling question using at least two sources from different positions. Required structure: claim; evidence; reasoning; one limitation or unanswered question. Students may write, annotate the map with an oral explanation, or record a short audio response.

\textbf{6. Exit connection --- 3 minutes.} Students propose one additional local source needed before claiming that a present environmental or educational pattern was caused by redlining. Possibilities include tree-canopy data, land-surface temperature, zoning records, investment records, or resident testimony.

\subsectiontitle{Discussion and assessment criteria}
The discussion question is open to interpretation but bounded by evidence. A strong response does not need to reach the teacher's preferred wording. It must (1) accurately source evidence; (2) distinguish institutional classification from community knowledge; (3) corroborate across at least two source types; (4) explain the reasoning linking evidence to the claim; and (5) state a real limit. During discussion, the teacher can be transparent that racist institutional language is not treated as an equally valid view of human worth while still inviting students to investigate how the system operated and what the sources establish.

\projectsection{4. Course Learning and Prior-Work Integration}
This task follows the intellectual progression of EDE477 rather than using the sources as illustrations for a predetermined conclusion. From Eisen and Wolfe-Rocca (2023), I take the principle that a timeline is a curated argument: the 1935--1940 entry makes federal housing systems visible, but the explicit limits prevent the timeline from pretending to contain the whole history. Wineburg (2001) and VanSledright (2015) support moving beyond present assumptions and helping students evaluate accounts rather than equating perspective with falsehood. Barton (2005) is especially important here: the usefulness of a primary source depends on the question, context, and corroboration, so the HOLC map is evidence of institutional judgment rather than transparent evidence of neighborhood value. Barton and Levstik (2015) support interpretation organized around a meaningful question and evidence-based public judgment.

The Class 4 verification work adds a contemporary layer. Caulfield and Wineburg's SIFT process asks students to stop, investigate the source, find stronger coverage, and trace claims to their original context. Applying SIFT to the WHEC article prevents a polished local report from becoming the only authority. Class 5 also matters: Hess (2015) frames discussion as structured inquiry, while Journell (2020) shows that source selection and framing communicate teacher choices even when a teacher claims neutrality. I therefore state the evidentiary boundary openly, provide competing positions and source types, and refuse false balance about racist human classifications.

The prior ED400/ED415 Resistance Mapping work contributes a local method: examine policy, map power, recover community knowledge, and keep resistance and agency visible. The prior RIT DSCI601 ethics and fairness work contributes a systems question: aggregate outcomes can hide patterned harm, and unequal access to useful context can itself produce unfair decisions. Those earlier ideas inform my EQUITAS/Puzzle Plan lens---who defines risk, who receives investment, whose knowledge becomes authoritative, and what evidence a fairer judgment requires. They are my application, not claims attributed to the EDE477 authors.

The same lens shapes whom I imagine when designing the inquiry. Students enter the classroom through multiple identities at once: culture, language, migration, race, neurodivergence, disability, chronic illness, family history, and prior encounters with institutions can shape how they access the same curriculum and whether their knowledge is recognized. Redlining is especially appropriate for this purpose because it shows how an institution can turn assumptions about people and place into apparently technical categories with unequal consequences. The classroom response must therefore examine institutional power while also refusing to reproduce it: community knowledge belongs beside official records, access barriers must not be mistaken for thinking barriers, and students need agency in how they demonstrate careful evidence-based judgment.
